Knives & Accessories

Essential blades for every task

Carving Fork

Knives are essential in food preparation for most people who cook almost everywhere in the world. Choosing which knives to buy can be difficult from the abundant choices — different shapes, sizes, blade materials and handle materials. Here is a quick look at some common knife shapes and their uses.

Common Knife Shapes

Knife Size Use
Chef Knife20–30 cm / 8–12"General purpose.
Santoku Knife8–12"Similar to Chef Knife, general purpose.
Paring Knife6–10 cm / 2.5–4"Small tasks, to peel and to core.
Serrated Knife13–20 cm / 5–8"Item with hard exterior and soft interior, such as bread.
Cleaver20–25 cm / 8–10"Tough edge, split bones, dense cartilage and thick meat.
Chinese Chef Knife15 cm / 6"General purpose.
Boning Knife18–22 cm / 7–8.5"Thin flexible blade, remove bones from meat.
Filleting Knife12–15 cm / 5–6"Very flexible, fillet and prepare fish.
Carving Knife15–28 cm / 6–11"Thinner than Chef Knife, cut slices of meat.

Blades are made of various materials including carbon steel, stainless steel, ceramics and plastics. Steel blades are generally considered better as they can be sharpened easily using a sharpening stone or whetstone and honed using a honing rod. Dull blades can be frustrating to use and are dangerous, as they can slip when failing to cut through, resulting in injury.

Handle materials should be chosen based on how safe they are. When handling a lot of greasy or slippery items, textured handles provide more grip. Common handle materials include wood, micarta and metal.

Cutting boards are usually made of plastic or wood — use a damp towel to prevent slipping. A meat fork holds meat when carving. A honing steel straightens the edge of a knife but does not sharpen it; a sharpening stone or whetstone sharpens the edge — the higher the grit number, the finer the finish.

Avoid putting knives close to each other to prevent dulling. Instead, store knives in knife blocks, on a magnetic strip, or in a knife roll.

03

Pans & Pots

Vessels for stovetop cooking

Before buying pans, it is important to consider what kind of food it will be used for cooking, the number of people it will be used for, and the capacity of the stove and oven. Cookware material is an important consideration as it has a drastic impact when cooking.

Cookware Materials

Material Advantages Disadvantages
AluminiumRelatively cheap, good heat conductor and lightweight.Reactive and can be stained. Anodised type is more stable.
Stainless SteelGood heat conductor, non-reactive and low maintenance.Relatively expensive and uneven heating surface.
Carbon SteelRelatively cheap, great heat conductor, non-stick after seasoning.High maintenance and highly reactive with acidic foods.
Cast IronRelatively cheap, great heat conductor and retainer. Oven safe.High maintenance and highly reactive with acidic foods.
CopperGreat heat conductor and even heat retention.Expensive, high maintenance and highly reactive.

Enamel, non-stick and ceramic refer to the coating only. Ceramic can withstand high heat but falls short in its non-stick properties. Enamel coating on cast iron helps with maintenance and allows cooking with acidic food without worrying too much.

Handle attachment affects heat transfer from the pan to the handle. Handles with similar material, albeit less heat conductive than the pan, are preferred. Riveted attachments tend to be sturdy and reduce heat transfer, while welded attachments produce a smoother finish with less food build-up. One-piece pans are seamless and often ergonomic, but transfer more heat to the handles.

07

Knife Skills

Techniques for safe cutting

A fair amount of time spent when cooking involves cutting ingredients. Getting comfortable using a knife properly will make it quicker, safer and more enjoyable to cook regularly. The two most important things to learn are to always use a sharp knife and to make sure to stabilise the cutting board. A dull knife requires more force to use and increases the chance of accidents, while not doing a great job at making the intended cuts.

Some cutting boards come with non-slip rubber feet, but most do not. Use a damp cloth, a damp kitchen towel, or non-slip shelf liner cut to size to make sure the cutting board will not move around.

Grips

Pinch Grip

Place the middle finger behind the knife bolster or guard, with the ring and little finger around the handle, while pinching the blade with the thumb and forefinger. This is safe even with slightly damp hands and provides optimal blade control.

Claw Grip

Hold on to the ingredient with the thumb and fingers, and keep the fingertips curled inward to avoid getting hit by the knife's blade. The side of the blade should rest against the furthest knuckle and stay perpendicular to the cutting board. The blade should not go higher than the guiding knuckle when cutting, regardless of the type of technique and size of ingredient used — with a few exceptions such as peeling, cutting some large vegetables to a more manageable size, and deboning meat.

Cutting Techniques

Slice

Food is cut lengthwise uniformly. The tip of the blade rests on the cutting board and the knife is angled upwards before slicing downward and forward into the food while keeping the tip of the blade in contact with the cutting board.

Chop

Similar to slicing but the whole blade is lifted up and cuts are made by downward and upward motion. It is not to be confused with the culinary cut rough chop, where food is cut into rough cubes that are similar in size.

Back Slice

Useful in making fine slices of herbs where other techniques can crush the ingredient instead of cutting. The tip of the blade is placed at an upward angle lower than the slicing technique and the knife is only pulled backward to make the cut.

Rock Chop

Used to finely mince herbs, garlic, ginger and other similar ingredients. The tip of the knife is placed at one side of the ingredient and is held securely with the free hand, and the knife is rocked up and down until the ingredient is minced to the desired size. Occasionally, the ingredients will need to be re-gathered.

Cooking Methods

Heat application techniques

There are numerous ways to cook food, and which method(s) to choose will depend on the ingredients being used, the final dish that needs to be achieved, the equipment available and personal preference. There are two main categories of cooking methods — dry heat cooking and moist heat cooking — as well as combination cooking, which combines the two.

Dry Heat Cooking

Method Description
Stir FrySmall amount of fat. Very high heat. Food stirred and tossed quickly.
SautéSmall amount of fat. High heat. Food often left to brown to develop a fond.
Pan FryVery small amount of fat. Medium heat for even cooking.
SweatSmall amount of fat. Low heat, often with added salt to draw moisture out without any browning.
Bain-marie / Double BoilerSimmering water in a saucepan or pot and a bowl with the ingredient(s) on top. To melt or cook delicate ingredients.
Shallow FryFood is partly submerged in cooking fat and turned over during cooking to cook other side(s).
Deep FryFood is fully submerged in cooking fat until fully cooked.
SmokeFood is exposed to smoke from smouldering material(s), usually wood or wood chips.
Bake / RoastIndirect and evenly distributed heat around the food. Baking is usually done at a lower temperature compared to roasting.
Broil / GrillDirect heat from either the top (broil) or bottom (grill) and food needs to be turned over to cook the other side(s).

Moist Heat Cooking

Method Description
SteamSimmering water in a saucepan or pot and a steamer on top. Bamboo steamers absorb excess moisture while metal steamers do not.
BlanchFood is boiled briefly and cooled down immediately into iced water or under cold running water to stop the cooking process.
ScaldThe liquid is heated until small bubbles start to form (around 83°C for milk).
PoachFood is cooked fully submerged in hot liquid, usually water (around 70–80°C).
SimmerFood is cooked fully submerged in very hot liquid, usually water (around 85–95°C). Small and infrequent bubbles will appear.
BoilFood is cooked fully submerged in very hot liquid, usually water (around 100°C). Large and very frequent bubbles will appear.
Steep / InfuseThe ingredient is soaked in liquid, usually water, to extract flavours or to soften the item. Infusion is the extraction into the liquid, often water, oil, or alcohol.

Combination Cooking

Method Description
BraiseFood is pan fried or sautéed at high temperature and then liquid up to 1/3 the height of the ingredients is added and cooked slowly.
StewFood may or may not be pan fried or sautéed at high temperature and then liquid fully covering the ingredients is added and cooked slowly.
Sous VideFood is vacuum-sealed and cooked in water at a constant temperature. Food such as steak requires a quick sear after sous vide.
